How Do Spies Send Secret Messages?

A 2,500-year mission through spies, codebreakers, and the codes that changed history. Then you crack a secret code yourself.

Mission briefing

Your objective

A message is only useful if the right person can read it and the wrong person can't. For thousands of years, people have invented sneaky ways to hide and scramble messages. Making secret codes is called cryptography, from the Greek word kryptos, meaning hidden. Breaking those codes makes you a codebreaker.

Agent, your first assignment: read the nine case files below, then crack the code in the decoder.
The case files

Nine files, 2,500 years

File 01500 BCE

The Shaved Messenger

According to the Greek historian Herodotus, a man needed to get a message past enemy soldiers. He shaved a messenger's head, tattooed the message on his scalp, and waited for his hair to grow back. Talk about the world's slowest text message!

File 02Ancient Sparta

The Scytale

Spartan generals wrapped a strip of leather around a wooden stick and wrote across it. Unwrapped, the strip looked like random letters. The only way to read it was to wrap it around another stick of the exact same size. The stick was the key.

File 03~50 BCE

Caesar's Shift

Julius Caesar moved every letter forward three spots in the alphabet. A became D, B became E, and so on. CAT becomes FDW. It's called the Caesar cipher, and puzzle books still use it today.

File 04~1,200 years ago · Baghdad

Al-Kindi Counts the Letters

Scholar Al-Kindi noticed that some letters appear far more often than others. In English, E is the most common. If a coded message has tons of H's, the H probably stands for E. Codebreakers still use this idea.

File 051586 · England

The Beer Barrel Letters

Mary, Queen of Scots, sent coded letters hidden inside a beer barrel, thinking they were safe. Queen Elizabeth's spymaster, Sir Francis Walsingham, had them read, and codebreaker Thomas Phelippes cracked the code. Code makers and codebreakers have been in a race ever since.

File 06American Revolution

The Culper Ring

General George Washington ran a secret spy network called the Culper Ring. They used invisible ink and a code book where numbers stood for people, places, and words. Washington himself was number 711. Legend says spy Anna Strong signaled with a black petticoat on her clothesline. Nobody knows if it's true, but it's one of the coolest spy stories ever told.

File 071917 · World War One

Room 40 Strikes

The Zimmermann Note was a secret German telegram written in code. British codebreakers in a hidden office called Room 40 cracked it. When it was revealed, it helped push the United States into the war.

Bonus intel: spy pigeons carried tiny cameras in the early 1900s.
File 08World War Two

Enigma & the Code Talkers

Germany's Enigma machine had about 159 quintillion possible settings. British codebreakers at Bletchley Park, including Alan Turing and many women, cracked it in secret, and many couldn't tell their own families. Meanwhile, Navajo Code Talkers sent messages in the Navajo language that the enemy never cracked.

File 09Cold War

The Wooden Seal Bug

In 1945, Soviet children gave the American ambassador in Moscow a carved wooden Great Seal of the United States. Hidden inside was a listening device with no batteries and no wires. It was powered by radio waves beamed from outside the building, and it wasn't discovered until 1952.

Experiment · Try at home

Make invisible ink

Real spies used invisible ink, and you can make your own with things from your kitchen.

01

Squeeze lemon juice into a small bowl and add a few drops of water.

02

Dip a cotton swab in the mix and write a secret message on white paper.

03

Let it dry completely. Your message is now invisible.

04

Ask a grown-up to gently warm the paper with a low-heat iron. Your message appears in brown.

warning Always have a grown-up handle the iron. Keep hot tools away from little fingers.

Why it works: lemon juice turns brown when it gets warm, and it weakens the paper a little where you wrote, so your message darkens before the rest of the page does.
